# Iron and steel masts — the diameter is a record question

*Round 69, 2026-08-10. The second half of the r68 mast-diameter question: r68 gave every wooden
mast its own tree (Steel 1794's inch-per-yard), but iron hulls kept the old `beam × 0.06`
calibration, which is the wooden law's number with the wooden law's justification removed. A
plated tube is not a tree; its diameter is whatever the builder rolled, and that is a record
question. The handoff said: check the records before touching it. These are the records.*

---

## 1. Great Eastern, 1858 — the one fully attested set

Source: the *Great Eastern* history at atlantic-cable.com (History of the Atlantic Cable &
Submarine Telegraphy), compiling the contemporary descriptions. Read in full text 2026-08-10.

> "The masts were constructed of iron with the exception of the stern mast (Saturday), which
> was of wood, as the compass was to be situated on or near this mast. The iron masts consisted
> of two plates, each formed into half circles and butt jointed with internal plates. Discs of
> wrought iron reinforced with angle iron were riveted inside to give additional strength.
> Sizes of the various masts were as follows. Heights given are from keel to truck.
> Monday, 2 feet 9 inches diameter, 172 feet high. Tuesday, 3 feet 6 inches diameter, 216 feet
> high. Wednesday, 3 feet 6 inches diameter, 225 feet high. Thursday 3 feet 6 inches diameter,
> 216 feet high. Friday, 2 feet 9 inches diameter, 188 feet high. Saturday, 2 feet 9 inches
> diameter, 164 feet high. From the keel to the upper deck they were encased in a square tube
> of iron plate. The lower yards of the square rigged masts were also made of iron and were
> 126 feet long and 2 feet 6 inches diameter at the centre. The stays were of 7½ inch wire
> rope with the exception of the stern mast where they were made of hemp."

Also from the same passage: six masts named Monday → Saturday bow to stern; **Tuesday and
Wednesday square rigged**, the rest fore-and-aft, Thursday equipped to carry square canvas.

In metres, with the ratio of keel-to-truck height to tube diameter:

| mast | material | diameter | keel–truck | H/D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | iron | 0.838 m | 52.4 m | 62.5 |
| Tuesday | iron | 1.067 m | 65.8 m | 61.7 |
| Wednesday | iron | 1.067 m | 68.6 m | 64.3 |
| Thursday | iron | 1.067 m | 65.8 m | 61.7 |
| Friday | iron | 0.838 m | 57.3 m | 68.4 |
| Saturday | **wood** | 0.838 m | 50.0 m | 59.6 |

The five iron tubes hold H/D between 61.7 and 68.4 on keel-to-truck height. Above the upper
deck (hull depth 58 ft) the ratio is 42–48. The one wooden mast is the stubbiest per length,
which is the direction wood should differ. Her iron lower yard: 126 ft / 2 ft 6 in = **L/D 50.4**.

## 2. Peking, 1911 — the steel yard law, from the re-masting itself

Source: Stabikon GmbH (the steel-forming contractor for the Peters-Werft restoration), project
page "Viermastbark Peking", delivery list for the 2017–2019 re-masting. Read 2026-08-10.

| spar | Ø slings/heel | Ø ends | length | L/D at slings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bowsprit | 631 mm | 308 mm | 18.8 m | 29.8 |
| Spanker boom | 260 mm | 235 mm | 16.0 m | 61.5 |
| 3 × topgallant stengen | 500 mm heel | 90 mm head | 20.0 m | 40.0 |
| 2 × lower yard | 574 mm | 287 mm | 28.7 m | **50.0** |
| 2 × lower topsail yard | 528 mm | 264 mm | 26.4 m | **50.0** |
| 3 × upper topsail yard | 508 mm | 254 mm | 25.4 m | **50.0** |
| 3 × lower topgallant yard | 440 mm | 220 mm | 22.0 m | **50.0** |
| 3 × upper topgallant yard | 390 mm | 195 mm | 19.5 m | **50.0** |
| 3 × royal yard | 296 mm | 148 mm | 14.8 m | **50.0** |

**Every steel yard is exactly length/50 at the slings, tapering to length/100 at the arms** —
that is the rule the restorers cut to, and Great Eastern's 1858 iron yard sits at 50.4 on the
same measure. One constant across 53 years and two materials of build. NOT yet applied to the
drawing — the drawn yard is `beam × 0.007`-ish and on iron ships that is several times too
thin; recorded as the next class for the survey.

Peking's mast heights (de.wikipedia, "Peking (Schiff)"): 62 m over keel, 51 m over deck. Her
mizzen is a pole mast, "Untermast und Stenge ein Stück". The delivery list contains stengen
and spars but no lower-mast tubes (the originals were restored in place), so her tube diameter
is not in this source.

## 3. Preussen, 1902 — structure attested, diameter not

Source: de.wikipedia "Preußen (Schiff, 1902)", citing Hamecher 1993; the full technical
description is W. Kaemmerer, *Das Fünfmast-Vollschiff Preußen*, Zeitschrift des VDI Bd. 48,
1904 — not reachable this round, and the place to look if the diameter is ever wanted first-hand.

- "Alle Masten und Spieren mit Ausnahme der Besangaffel waren aus Stahlrohr gefertigt" — all
  masts and spars of steel tube except the wooden spanker gaff.
- "Masten (Untermasten und Marsstengen ein Stück) mit Bramstenge" — **lower mast and topmast
  are one tube**, with a separate topgallant stenge above.
- Mast height 68 m keel–truck, **58 m deck–truck**. Main yard 32 m, royal yard 16 m.

The model's drawn pole for her mains (30 m lower × 1.9 segment stack) is 57 m — the data
already agrees with the record's 58.

## 4. Titanic, 1912 — height attested, diameter bounded

- en.wikipedia "Titanic": "Two masts, each 155 ft (47 m) high, supported derricks for working
  cargo."
- The foremast is a hollow steel tube climbed from inside — the lookouts reached the crow's
  nest by an internal ladder (widely attested; the nest's doorway is visible on the wreck's
  fallen mast). That wants a bore most of a metre at the base.
- No diameter found in sources reachable this round. Beveridge et al., *Titanic: The Ship
  Magnificent* Vol. I, is where a stated figure would live.

## 5. The law as applied, and its label

- **Attested wins.** A mast record carrying `diaM` (metres) is drawn at that diameter. This
  round only Great Eastern's six masts are attested, so only they carry it.
- **Derived elsewhere: tube diameter = drawn pole length above deck / 55.** Calibrated on the
  Great Eastern pairs whose model heights match the record best (Wednesday 57, Tuesday 54 on
  the model's own measure); the model's Monday/Friday heights are flatter than the record's
  ratio, which is a height question, not a diameter one. The constant is DERIVED and each
  derived card says so.
- **One tube for a steel square-rigger's lower+topmast from 1890** (Preussen's attested
  construction; standard late-windjammer practice), so the drawn topmast segment continues at
  the tube's diameter. Earlier iron ships (the 1870 steamer, Great Eastern's gaff rig) keep a
  separate, thinner sent-down topmast — which is what the white-lower/black-upper livery
  joint already marks.
- **The topgallant stenge heel is 0.48 of the tube** — Peking's Ø500 mm stengen against a
  tube that must exceed them, and the drawn result puts Preussen's stenge at 0.50 m. Derived.
- Domain: applied to `iron: true` hulls only. Yamato, Dreadnought, QM2 have no reachable
  diameter record at all; they take the derived law and the derived label (rule 10 — a
  fallback the UI names).

## 7. Candidates surfaced by this research, NOT done this round

1. **Steel yards are length/50 at the slings, length/100 at the arms** — attested twice
   (Peking 1911 exact on six spar classes, Great Eastern 1858 at 50.4). The drawn iron-ship
   yards are several times too thin. Next class for the survey.
   → **DONE round 70**, fleet-wide (the wooden rates read in Steel 1794 the same round):
   see `Research/YARDS.md`.
2. **Great Eastern's rig is wrong in kind**: Tuesday and Wednesday were square-rigged (the
   record above), the model draws all six gaff. Also the aftermost mast is wood (the compass),
   hemp-stayed.
3. **Iron hulls draw no bowsprit** (`buildBowsprit` returns on `S.iron`) — Preussen's
   klüverbaum is the spar she lost in the 1910 collision; she should carry one.
4. QM2's mast data (two poles at Titanic's exact stations and 9.46° rake) looks copied from
   Titanic; her real fit is a single signal mast atop the bridge. Needs its own record pass.
5. Great Eastern's model mast-height curve (34/38/40/40/38/32) is flatter than the record's
   (172/216/225/216/188/164 ft) — Monday and Friday are drawn too tall relative to Wednesday.

# r187 — the sekibune's yotsume-ikari: the anchor her own plate draws at the barrier's end

The r186 queue head, the anchors line's last member: "the wasen yotsume-ikari
(four-fluke iron anchor, minamichita)", named alongside the fleet's other anchors at
r175 and unexamined since. The candidate hull is the sekibune — the fleet's one wasen.
The shadow over the item was r176: the same museum's rokuro was judged SILENT for this
hull, because every attestation was the Edo cargo tradition and the drawn ship is a
1597 Sengoku warship. The anchor question opens under the same discipline and closes
the other way, because the evidence points the other way — at her class, at her era,
and on her own plate.

## What each source can support — and what it cannot

1. **The name and the popular form** — 南知多町 和船文化 (Minamichita wasen museum),
   minamichita-museum.com/wasen/ship.html, the 上貫木 item, verbatim:
   「碇は、『四爪碇（よつづめいかり）』と呼ばれるもので、鉄の角棒の先端を四つ割りにして
   四方に曲げて爪を作ったためこの名で呼ばれ、船の大きさによってその使用数などは
   異なっていました。」 A square iron bar, its tip split in four and the quarters bent
   outward into claws; the count varying with the ship's size. Subject: the Edo
   弁才船 — this source alone is the rokuro's evidence over again, and alone it would
   end the same way.

2. **The era and the class** — 松井広信「四爪鉄錨の基礎的研究 — 船に関わるモノの型式学的
   考察」金沢大学考古学紀要 34 (2013) 27–52, kanazawa-u.repo.nii.ac.jp record 6439,
   fetched WHOLE this round (PDF in this directory). Page 27, carrying 石井謙治 1983
   (図説和船史話 322–326):
   - Born in Ming China; excavated Chinese examples include one inscribed 洪武五年
     (1372), and — pinning the r184 residual in passing — 蓬莱水城出土 1984: 全長
     2.15 m, 456 kg; 泉州四湖港出土 1981: 残高 2.68 m, 758.3 kg (fig. 3 caption).
   - In Japan: the four-claw iron anchor drawn in the 1433 『神功皇后縁起絵巻』, and
     the 1486 『戊子入明記』 recording 鉄錨 on the Ming embassy ships. Ishii's
     conclusion, verbatim: 「少なくとも15世紀前半には軍船などの特殊船に木石碇とともに
     四爪鉄錨が併用されていた」 — by the first half of the 15th century, WARSHIPS and
     special ships carried the four-claw iron anchor alongside wood-stone anchors.
   - General diffusion waited: the early-17th-century 『川口遊廓図屏風』 shows ONE
     four-claw iron anchor against EIGHT wood-stone anchors on ordinary Osaka wasen;
     Ishii puts the spread to the 廻船 at mid-17th century.
   **The class sentence points at her.** The sekibune of 1597 is a daimyo navy's
   warship — the named early-adopter class, 164 years after the first depiction —
   while the wood-stone mainstream belongs to the ordinary wasen she is not.

3. **Presence, DIRECT-BY-PLATE — the round's find.** The card's own plate (the Busan
   boat-barrier scroll of 1593, Univ. of Tokyo; local copy
   web/data/assets/ships/sekibune.jpg, 2095×2167) DRAWS the ground tackle: the barrier
   line of sekibune rides to twisted cables, and at the line's upper and lower ends
   the anchor itself is drawn at the cable's end — a stockless anchor, short shank,
   FOUR strongly recurved claws splayed from the crown (crop read this round:
   plate-anchor-top.png; the lower one is clipped at the sheet edge,
   plate-anchor-bottom2.png). The anchored fleet, this hull class, 1593 — four years
   before her drawn year, working exactly the recurved multi-claw stockless form the
   tradition names yotsume. The plate resolves FORM and PRESENCE only: at ~16 px/m
   (the card's own stated scale) no dimension can be taken from it, and none is (the
   Azzam rule). The cable is drawn as a twisted line; every fetched wasen source
   cordages the era in rope, so it is read as a laid warp, not chain.

4. **The forging process and the measured corpus** — Matsui again: 田村善次郎 1978's
   folkloric study of the 鞆 anchor smiths (the スド bars forge-welded, split, the
   claws spread; カン the ring), and Table 1's 49 surviving examples, full lengths
   1.05–3.03 m, with part-by-part measurements: 大野湊神社 a — 全長 269 cm, anchor
   ring 44 × 19 cm of 3 cm bar; b — 247 cm, ring 32 × 16.5 of 2.5 cm; small class
   1.05–1.55 m. Fig. 1 gives the part names drawn here: アンカーリング (the elongated
   head ring), 付属リング (the free ring through it the cable bends to), シャンク,
   アーム with フルーク tips. Nearly all are Edo–Meiji survivors: they support the
   FORM and the proportion defaults, not a Sengoku dimension.

5. **The weight calibration** — Matsui p.34, the 神津島沖海底遺跡: a raised four-claw
   anchor of 全長 2.8 m weighed by forklift at 330–340 kg, a second on the seabed at
   2.2 m. The Japanese form is a slender bar — far lighter per metre than the Chinese
   (Penglai: 2.15 m, 456 kg).

Not fetched, named: the 1433 emaki itself (誉田八幡宮, print/shrine — its warship
anchor depiction would be the direct era plate); 石井謙治 1983 in print; the Tenchi-maru
model's ground tackle (Museum of Maritime Science).

## The judgment: DRAWN, and every standing named

Warrant: presence DIRECT-BY-PLATE (source 3 — her own card's plate draws the anchor in
her own fleet, at anchor, 1593), form ERA+CLASS ATTESTED (source 2 — the four-claw
iron anchor on warships from the first half of the 15th century; the plate's own
recurved multi-claw stockless form agrees; the tradition's name and forging, sources
1 and 4). This is a stronger stack than r186's panokseon (DIRECT-BY-ALBUM at one
remove) — the plate is on her card already.

Standings:
- **Length 2.0 m, DRAWN CLASS DEFAULT** — inside the Matsui corpus (1.05–3.03 m), at
  the corpus's own small/large boundary (its 付属リング analysis breaks at 2 m). By
  the Kōzushima calibration, (2.0/2.8)³ × 335 ≈ 120 kg — a load a ~70-hand crew
  weighs by direct pull, consistent with her own card's rokuro row (JUDGED SILENT,
  r176: "hoisting by the crew's direct pull"). No sekibune anchor survives; any
  measured warship yotsume replaces this and the provenance says so.
- **Proportions, FIGURE/CORPUS DEFAULTS** — ring 0.16 × 0.08 of length (the 大野湊
  ratios), accessory ring free through it, square-bar shank (the museum's 角棒), four
  arms at 0.30 of length read off Matsui fig. 1's own proportions.
- **Count ONE, NOT RECORDED** — the museum says count varied by ship size; Ishii's
  sentence has iron ALONGSIDE wood-stone (併用) on the warships; no count or mix is
  attested for a sekibune. One is drawn (the presence the plate warrants); the mixed
  tackle is named in the provenance and not drawn.
- **Stow, PLACEMENT INFERENCE** — the fleet rule (r182–r186): recovered, lying on the
  open foredeck forward of the sō-yagura (the deck the cable would run out over the
  low bow from), rolled 45°, pitched to the deck's own gradient, settled by measured
  box. No belay is attested and she has no winding machine (r176): the cable is bent
  to the accessory ring and FLAKED IN A COIL beside it — a stow, not an invented
  lead (the r182/r185 standing). Coil from rope segments, never a torus: the audit
  counts anchors by ring tori.

## The change, class not instance

- hull.js buildFittings: a generic datum-driven `yotsumeAnchor` block — silence draws
  nothing; only a record draws one. Own PARTS card entry.
- vessels.json sekibune: `yotsumeAnchor{atU, offZ, yaw, lenM 2.0}` +
  `anchorProvenance` (this judgment) + a "Ground tackle, as drawn" row (her card is
  already array-form — the r186 panokseon residual does not recur here).
- audit-hulls.js: new rule, both-ways V-WARRANT; V-YARMS (4 fluke tips structurally);
  V-YRING (exactly 2 ring tori per anchor — head ring and accessory ring — kept
  distinct from ia-ring); V-YLEN (longest extent read from the anchor's own geometry
  through the world matrix scale — the r186 lesson: a world AABB cannot read a rolled
  stow); V-YREST (ray straight down, first non-anchor hit is the surface it must rest
  on — the r185 surface-asked form); V-YCABLE (cable endpoints at the ring and coil).
- Injection proofs on final code: sever (arms stripped under a faithful record →
  exactly one V-YARMS conviction); drag (shank stretched 1.3× → V-YLEN reads it
  through the stow transform).

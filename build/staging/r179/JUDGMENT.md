# r179 — the trireme windlass judgment, the last of the r172 line

## Judgment: SILENT — and a third shape, stronger than both precedents

r176 (sekibune) went silent because drawing the machine meant stacking three
inferences over a rokuro attested only in the tradition's later era. r178 (dhow)
went silent because the tradition attests no machine at any documented point —
nothing to extend. The trireme is neither shape. Hers is a third: **the complete
inventory that omits the machine.** Her own navy kept stone-cut inventories of
its dockyards — the fullest gear record of any ancient ship class — listing each
warship's ENTIRE equipment (ἐντελῆ σκεύη), and no winding machine is among it.
This silence is not absence of evidence; the list is positive evidence of the
whole equipment, and the machine is not in it. It is r177's warrant inverted:
the corbita DREW because her ship's own gear list (Lucian's one sentence) names
the machine; the trireme ends SILENT because her class's own gear lists — far
fuller than Lucian's sentence — omit it. The same class of source, obeyed both
ways.

## The evidence, fetched and grepped this round

1. **The Athenian naval inventories, via Torr 1894** (downloaded whole r177,
   build/staging/r177/torr-ancient-ships.txt, 548 KB; grepped again this round).
   - windlass|capstan|winch in the whole book: **1 match** (p. 95), and it is
     the sail-tackle of the big Roman merchantmen — Lucian's Isis, Plutarch's
     steersmen — the corbita's own r177 evidence, not the trireme's.
   - The entire-gear lists (CIA II = IG II², nos. 807, 808, 809, 811; 330/329 BC
     and after; nos. 793–794 for 357/6–356/5): every three- and four-banked ship
     carries ἀγκύρας σιδηρᾶς δύο — two iron anchors — and eight cables in two
     recorded sizes (ὀκτωδάκτυλα and ἑξδάκτυλα, four each; the earlier lists
     name them ἀγκύρεια, the anchor-cables, and ἐπίγυα, the shore-cables that
     moored her stern to the beach). Torr pp. 72–74, notes 161, 166, quoted
     verbatim in the fetched text.
   - The inventory WEIGHS the anchors: CIA II 807 col. b ll. 83–88 (329 BC),
     "ἀγκύρας σιδηρ[ᾶς, σταθμὸν μναῖ ΔΔ..]" — twenty-odd mnas; Torr's reading
     (note 167 + p. 70): had it exceeded 50 mnas the numeral would differ, and
     50 mnas is "rather less than 50 lbs" — "an anchor of less than half a
     hundred-weight being in use in the Athenian navy." Under ~25 kg. The
     reading is Torr's, named as his.
   - The hypozomata sit among the σκεύη κρεμαστά (hanging gear) in the same
     lists (Torr note 100, p. 42); no tensioning machine is listed with them.

2. **The Greek word itself — LSJ s.v. στροφεῖον** (lsj.gr, fetched whole,
   staging/lsj-stropheion.html): the "windlass, capstan" sense has exactly ONE
   nautical literary attestation — **Luc. Nav. 5** (c. AD 165, the Isis, r177's
   warrant) — and its classical-era epigraphic use is **IG XI(2).138 B 8
   (Delos, iv/iii BC)**: στροφεῖα as "dues payable by ships for the USE of a
   windlass, i.e. harbour-dues." The machine stood on the quay; ships paid to
   come to it.

3. **The machine in her own year — Herodotus 7.36.3** (Perseus, Greek and
   English fetched, staging/hdt736-*.html): the Hellespont bridge cables of
   480 BC — the drawn hull's own year (hull.year −480), laid over pontoon
   triremes and penteconters — were made taut **ἐκ γῆς**, from the land,
   **ὄνοισι ξυλίνοισι**, with wooden windlasses ("they stretched the cables
   from the land, twisting them taut with wooden windlasses"). Her world knew
   the machine; its place was the shore.

4. **Nothing for it to do aboard.** Two anchors of under ~25 kg; some 200
   hands; a 47 t displacement hull that moored stern-to with dedicated shore
   cables (the four ἐπίγυα of her own lists) and was hauled out on the
   ship-sheds' slipways. The trireme was the thing the shore's machines and
   men hauled — not a platform that hauled.

## Why not draw

Drawing a windlass would contradict the class's own complete inventory, not
extend it. Every prior draw in this line had a record to obey: r173 the wreck's
own machine, r174 two primary texts, r175 the navy album + living tradition,
r177 the ship's own gear list. Here the record answers the other way.

## Stated caveats (in the provenance)

- The entire-gear lists are the 4th-century navy's, ~150 years after the drawn
  instance (−480). They are the class's fullest record at ANY date, and the
  5th-century evidence (Herodotus' shore windlasses) points the same way; but
  the century gap is stated, not hidden.
- The anchor-weight reading (under 50 mnas) is Torr's restoration-reading of a
  damaged line, named as his.
- Scope: the judgment is for the classical trireme as inventoried by the
  Athenian navy. The giant polyremes of the Hellenistic age are different ships;
  their gear is not read from these lists. (Moschion's forty — via Athenaeus —
  carried four wooden and eight iron anchors; nothing about her machines is
  extended backward here either.)
- That the crew worked anchors and hypozomata by direct pull is an INFERENCE,
  marked as one on the card.

# r177 — the corbita windlass judgment: the Isis's own gear list, extended down her own trade

The r172 windlass residual's queue head (corbita, first of the three remaining after
r176's sekibune silence). Opened this round, judgment first. No prior research existed
for this hull's windlass; the r172 removal took away a Georgian capstan drawn by class
rule with no record. All sources below fetched THIS round.

## What each source can support — and what it cannot

1. **The direct attestation, in era** — Lucian, *Navigium* 5 (c. AD 165), the
   Alexandrian grain freighter *Isis* lying at Piraeus, in the gear list her admirer
   walks through, verbatim (Perseus, Jacobitz text; confirmed letter-for-letter by
   Torr 1894 p. 95 n. 205):
   «αἱ ἄγκυραι καὶ στροφεῖα καὶ περιαγωγεῖς καὶ αἱ κατὰ τὴν πρύμναν οἰκήσεις,
   θαυμάσια πάντα μοι ἔδοξε» — the anchors, the στροφεῖα and the περιαγωγεῖς, and
   the stern cabins. The topostext translation renders the pair "the capstans and
   windlasses" (search snippets of the Fowler translation agree; the sacred-texts
   page itself 403'd); Torr reads them as the ship's windlasses. The two nouns are
   winding machines in a working inventory, listed WITH the anchors — that much the
   text carries. **Which noun is the horizontal machine and which the vertical it
   does not say, and no fetched lexicon page rendered (Perseus LSJ pages served
   navigation only; lsj.gr 403) — named as a gap; the reading rests on Torr and the
   fetched translation, which agree.**

2. **The Latin parallel, two centuries earlier** — Lucretius, *De rerum natura*
   4.901–906 (fetched whole, thelatinlibrary): «ventus ... trudit agens magnam magno
   molimine navem / et manus una regit ... atque gubernaclum contorquet quo libet
   unum, / multaque per trocleas et tympana pondere magno / commovet atque levi
   sustollit machina nisu.» The ship is explicit (navem, gubernaclum); the machine
   with its pulleys and drums lifts great weights at light effort in the same
   breath. Torr p. 95 n. 205: "These trochleae and tympana are probably the
   στροφεῖα and περιαγωγεῖς, for the context is about a ship." A scholarly reading,
   named as such — Lucretius alone does not put the machine on deck.

3. **Working context** — Plutarch, *praecepta gerendae reipublicae* 15 (Torr n. 205):
   steersmen do some work with their own hands, "and some with other instruments,
   sitting at a distance, they turn and wind" (περιάγουσι καὶ στρέφουσι) — the two
   machine nouns' own verb roots, aboard ship.

4. **Where the anchor work is** — Torr 1894 pp. 69–74 (fetched whole, archive.org
   full text): the bow eyes "turned to account as hawse-holes for the anchor-cables";
   "the anchors used to be suspended from the catheads a little way abaft of these
   hawse-holes" (Eur. *IT* 1350–1, Pindar *Pyth.* 4.191–2 cited); the Athenian navy
   lists four cables "to serve the two anchors at the bows", the stern set being
   shore-cables; anchors could be put out astern at need (Acts 27:29 in Torr's
   citations). **The bower anchors and their cables work at the BOW. No ancient
   source places the winding machine itself** — Torr p. 95 reads "various windlasses
   about the ship" serving the larger ropes generally.

5. **The class bridge is the record's own card** — the drawn corbita is 40 × 9 m at
   AD 200: the Madrague de Giens size, and the card's own rows carry both that wreck
   and the *Isis* herself (55 m, Lucian's numbers) as instances of this class. The
   extension runs from the class's giant, thirty-five years before the drawn year,
   down the same Alexandria–Rome trade to its 40 m standard carrier — ONE stated
   inference (size/typicality), same era, same tradition, same function. Contrast
   r176, where the same question stacked three (era, class, placement) and rightly
   ended silent.

## What is NOT drawn, and the negative evidence

- **No capstan.** If περιαγωγεῖς is a distinct vertical machine, its Roman FORM is
  unattested; Falconer's Georgian capstan (whelps, drumhead, pawls) on this hull was
  exactly the r172 anachronism. One horizontal windlass is drawn; the provenance says
  the second word exists and is not drawn.
- **The one wreck preserved to the rail is silent.** Laurons 2 (late 3rd c. AD,
  ~15 m, ~30 t local coaster; Gassend–Liou–Ximénès, Archaeonautica 1984, first page
  via Persée; Trier Laurons II project page) preserves bitts and belaying pins and a
  spare rudder — no windlass in any fetched description. She is the class's SMALL
  end; the drawn hull is not her size. Stated in the provenance as the standing
  negative.
- **No fetched relief or mosaic shows the machine.** The card's steering rows lean on
  the Sidon and Portus reliefs; neither is claimed here for the windlass.
- **Casson (SSAW 1971) not readable** — archive.org copy borrow-gated, search-inside
  blocked; Google Books exposed a single hit at p. 416 with no snippet text. Named
  as not fetched, the r174 Needham/Worcester shape.

## The judgment

DRAW, warrant **GEAR-LIST EXTENSION (same trade, same era)** — direct textual
attestation of winding machines among the anchors' own gear on the class's largest
instance, AD ~165, extended to the drawn AD 200 instance of the same trade. Placement
at the bow is a **PLACEMENT INFERENCE**, stated: the machine serves gear that works
at the bow (source 4), and no text places it; drawn on the foredeck abaft the
artemon's step. Dimensions: station a placement inference ±0.05 u; barrel length a
GEOMETRIC read off the drawn deck at the station (probe-bow, read-only, before any
record was written); diameter, axis height, standards Falconer CLASS DEFAULTS
cross-tradition, as r173–r175. Scope: a Roman-form vertical winch, if ever attested
in form, would add the second machine; a corbita drawn at Laurons 2's own size would
reopen the question toward silence.

# r178 — the dhow windlass judgment: a tradition with no machine to extend

## The question

The r172 residual, queue head after r177 (corbita drawn, sekibune silent): does the
sewn-plank dhow get a windlass? The drawn ship is the sewn early type — c. AD 830,
the Belitung wreck's tradition, 26 m loa, no iron in her fastenings — per the record's
own steeringProvenance (r121), which already fixed the reading: the stern-hung rudder
and the big late types (baghlah, boum) are the card's burden rows, not the drawn ship.

## The judgment: SILENT

The r176 sekibune stacked three inferences (era, class, placement) over a machine
that at least EXISTED in its tradition's later era. The dhow's case is stronger:
**no fetched source attests a windlass, winch or capstan anywhere in the dhow
tradition at any documented point** — not on the drawn ship's own wreck, not in the
tradition's 7th–16th-c. survey, not at its 19th–20th-c. documented end, where the
anchors hang on the kalba and the winch belongs to the engine era. There is no
machine to extend across an era or class gap; drawing one would invent its
existence, its form and its place at once — the exact class of error the r172
Georgian capstan was (a machine from another tradition drawn on silence).

What the tradition attests instead is the hand: anchors light enough to stow loose
on deck or hang at the prow beam (the Belitung grapnel's shank was WOOD; the Omani
stone anchors 40–50 kg), rope hawsers, bitts and a bollard, and crews of twelve to
thirty (the card's own crew row). That the drawn ship's crew worked her grapnel by
direct pull is the extension backward from the documented late practice — an
inference, marked as one on the card.

## Scope

The judgment is for the drawn sewn trader and holds for the sail tradition as
documented. A powered conversion's winch (catnaps' own modern craft) is a different
ship. A fetched attestation of a sail-era machine reopens it.
